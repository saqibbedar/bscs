— LAYER 01 — The Physical Layer

The layer that moves raw bits across a wire, fibre, or radio wave — converting 1s and 0s into signals the medium can physically carry.

Real-world analogy Think of the Physical Layer as the postman on a motorcycle delivering envelopes. He does not read the letter, does not know the sender or recipient, does not check if it reached the right person — his single job is to physically move the envelope from one post box to the next. Everything about the road, the fuel, the vehicle, the speed, and the handover between post boxes — that's the Physical Layer.

§ 01 Core Responsibilities

The slides explicitly list four duties of the Physical Layer. Each is expanded below.

  • Physical characteristics of interfaces and media Defines the mechanical and electrical specifications of the interface between a device and the transmission medium — connector shape, pin count, pin usage, cable type (copper, coax, fibre, air), voltage levels, and impedance. Example: the RJ-45 connector, the DB-9 serial port, the specification that a "1" is +5V and "0" is 0V on a particular interface.
  • Representation of bits (Encoding & Modulation) A bit is a logical concept — but a wire only understands voltage, and a fibre only understands pulses of light. The Physical Layer encodes each bit into a physical signal. This is done by line encoding (e.g., NRZ, Manchester, 4B/5B) for digital signals and by modulation (ASK, FSK, PSK, QAM) when converting to analog for transmission.
  • Data rate (bit duration) Defines how long a bit lasts on the medium. If the data rate is 1 Mbps, each bit occupies 1/1,000,000 of a second. This is the bit interval — and the receiver must sample the signal during this interval to recover the bit.
  • Synchronization of bits Sender and receiver clocks must agree on when each bit starts and ends. If the receiver samples too early or too late, it reads garbage. Synchronization can be achieved through dedicated clock signals, self-clocking encodings (Manchester), or training preambles at the start of a frame.

Two additional responsibilities appear across the slides that an examiner will expect you to state:

  • Line Configuration — point-to-point or multipoint connections (from Lec 2, Slide 7).
  • Physical Topology — mesh, star, bus, ring, hybrid (how devices are physically interconnected).
  • Transmission Mode — simplex, half-duplex, or full-duplex (direction of signal flow).

§ 02 Detailed Concepts

2.1 What is a "bit" at this layer?

At every layer above Layer 1, data has structure — segments, packets, frames. Here, all structure is gone. The Physical Layer sees only a stream of 1s and 0s. It does not know where a frame starts, what a header means, or who the destination is. Its contract with Layer 2 is: "Hand me bits. I will push them out. On the other side, I'll hand bits to the peer Layer 2."

2.2 Transmission Modes (Direction of Data Flow)

Simplex

One-way only. One device transmits, the other only receives. Example: keyboard → CPU, traditional broadcast TV.

Half-Duplex

Both devices can transmit and receive — but not at the same time. Communication must alternate. Example: walkie-talkie. When one talks, the other listens.

Full-Duplex

Both devices can transmit and receive simultaneously. Example: telephone call, modern Ethernet over twisted pair.

Why this matters

The transmission mode constrains how the physical medium is wired (separate send/receive channels for full-duplex) and how the Data Link Layer must coordinate access.

2.3 Data Representation (inputs to the Physical Layer)

All forms of data reaching the Physical Layer have already been reduced to bits by higher layers. The slides list five representations that you should be able to recall:

  • Text — represented as a bit pattern; common codes: ASCII, Unicode.
  • Numbers — represented by their binary equivalent.
  • Images — bit patterns representing pixels (and colour depth per pixel).
  • Audio — continuous signal sampled and quantised into bits (e.g., PCM).
  • Video — sequence of image frames plus audio, compressed (e.g., H.264).

2.4 Physical Topologies (from Lec 1 & Lec 2)

The arrangement of physical links between devices. The slides cover five, listed below with properties drawn directly from the deck.

TopologyLinksAdvantagesDisadvantages
Mesh Dedicated point-to-point to every other device. n(n-1)/2 links; each node needs n-1 I/O ports. Dedicated links; robust; security & privacy; easy fault identification and isolation. Bulk of wiring; high I/O port count; expensive; difficult to install and reconnect.
Star Every device connected by dedicated point-to-point link to a central controller (hub/switch). Less expensive than mesh; easy to install and configure; robust; easy fault isolation. More cabling than ring/bus; single point of failure at the hub.
Bus Multipoint — one backbone cable links all devices via drop lines & taps. Less cabling; easy to install and reconfigure. Limited number of taps; difficult reconnection & fault isolation; a break in bus stops all transmission.
Ring Dedicated point-to-point to neighbours; each device functions as a repeater; signal passes device to device. Less cabling; easy to install and reconfigure. A break in the ring can disable the entire network.
Hybrid Any combination of the above (e.g., a backbone bus of stars). Flexibility; scales large deployments; inherits strengths of combined topologies. Complex to design; troubleshooting combines issues of multiple topologies.

2.5 Packet Switching vs Circuit Switching (Physical/Link interaction)

Although switching is not exclusively a Physical Layer concern, the slides introduce it here because it dictates how the physical channel is used.

  • Circuit Switching — a dedicated end-to-end communication path is established between two stations before data flows. The path is a sequence of links. Switches must know the route and allocate bandwidth (channel) to establish a connection. Example: traditional telephone network.
  • Packet Switching — messages are divided into fixed-sized, numbered packets that are independently routed across the network. Devices called routers direct packets between networks. No dedicated channel — efficiency comes from statistical sharing. Example: the Internet.

2.6 Transmission Media Connection Technologies

The slides list the home-to-Internet connection technologies as Physical Layer examples:

  • Phone modem — converts digital computer data into an analog audio signal for transmission over a telephone line; a modem at the destination converts it back.
  • DSL (Digital Subscriber Line) — uses regular copper phone lines to carry digital data to and from the phone company's central office.
  • Cable modem — uses the same coaxial cable line as cable TV to transfer data.
  • Broadband — any connection faster than 128 bps (slide definition). DSL and cable are broadband. Download and upload speeds often differ (asymmetric).

Fig. 1.1 — The Physical Layer contract: bits in, signals on the wire, bits out.
Step-by-step. (1) The Data Link Layer hands a framed bit stream down. (2) Layer 1 encodes those bits using a line encoding scheme (NRZ, Manchester, 4B/5B…) or modulates a carrier wave. (3) The signal travels across the medium — experiencing attenuation, noise, delay. (4) On the destination side, Layer 1 samples the incoming signal at the correct bit interval and reconstructs the bits. (5) Those bits are passed up to the peer Data Link Layer.
What the examiner wants you to see The Physical Layer does not know about frames, addresses, or protocols. It is a dumb, reliable pipe for bits. Any question that asks "what is the unit at Layer 1" must be answered with bit, not frame or packet.

How to read it. Each letter is a host (node). In Mesh, every node links directly to every other — count the lines: for 4 nodes, 4(4-1)/2 = 6 links. In Star, all nodes route through a central hub H. In Bus, all nodes tap a single shared backbone cable. In Ring, each node links only to two neighbours forming a closed loop. In Hybrid, two star clusters are joined by a backbone — the most common real-world form.
What the examiner wants you to see Be able to compute n(n-1)/2 for mesh. Be able to justify robustness: mesh wins; a single cable break disables ring or bus; a hub failure kills a star. Always pair the topology with at least one advantage and one disadvantage from the slide — that's the Cisco-style answer pattern.

Diagram 3.3 — Packet vs Circuit Switching

Fig. 1.3 — Circuit vs packet switching as presented in Lec 1.
Step-by-step. In circuit switching, before any data moves, the network reserves a channel from A to B end-to-end — bandwidth is guaranteed but wasted if nobody talks. In packet switching, the message is chopped into numbered packets; each packet travels independently through the routers and may arrive out-of-order; the receiver reassembles using the packet numbers.
What the examiner wants you to see Circuit → dedicated path, must know route & allocate bandwidth, example: telephone. Packet → efficient sharing, numbered packets, routers do the directing, example: the Internet. Mentioning both the efficiency argument and the routing device is what separates a half-mark answer from a full-mark answer.

Standards organisations that govern Layer 1

  • ISO — publishes the OSI reference model itself.
  • ITU-T — telecom standards (V.90 modem, G.992 ADSL).
  • ANSI — US-level standards (FDDI).
  • IEEE — Ethernet (802.3), Wi-Fi (802.11), Bluetooth (802.15).
  • EIA — physical connectors (RS-232, RS-485).

The two types of standards the slides explicitly name are de jure (by law/regulation, legislated by an official body) and de facto (by convention or widespread use).

§ 05 Data Handling

Data unit

At Layer 1 the Protocol Data Unit (PDU) is the BIT. Nothing else.

Encapsulation role

The Physical Layer does not add a header or trailer. It receives the complete frame from the Data Link Layer (which has already added its header/trailer) and converts that entire bit stream into physical signals. On the receiving side, it reverses the process and hands the bit stream up.

Fig. 1.4 — The full encapsulation chain. Layer 1 carries the entire frame as bits.

Cross-layer flow

  1. Sender side: bits from L2 → encoded into signal → placed on medium.
  2. Medium: signal propagates (suffers attenuation, noise, delay).
  3. Receiver side: signal sampled at each bit interval → decoded to bits → handed up to L2.

§ 06 Must-Remember Points

High-Priority Facts

  • PDU at Layer 1 = BIT. Never frame, never packet.
  • Four duties from the slide: physical characteristics, representation of bits, data rate, synchronization of bits.
  • Three transmission modes: simplex, half-duplex, full-duplex.
  • Mesh formula: n(n-1)/2 links, each node needs n-1 I/O ports.
  • Devices that operate at Layer 1: repeaters, hubs, cables, connectors, modems, transceivers — they deal only in signals.
  • Broadband (slide definition) = connection faster than 128 bps — a DSL or cable modem qualifies.
  • Packet switching uses routers; circuit switching reserves a dedicated path.

Common Exam Traps

Trap 1: Confusing the Physical Layer with the Data Link Layer. A hub is Layer 1. A switch is Layer 2. Remember: if the device reads a MAC address, it's Layer 2.
Trap 2: Answering "packets" when asked about the data unit at Layer 1. Wrong. Bits.
Trap 3: Mixing up topology (physical) with mode (direction of flow). Star is a topology; half-duplex is a mode.
Trap 4: Citing protocols like TCP or IP as examples. Those are higher layers. At Layer 1 cite cable specs, encoding schemes, or IEEE 802.3/.11 physical specs.

Memory Shortcuts

  • The 4 Duties = "P-R-D-S" → Physical characteristics · Representation of bits · Data rate · Synchronisation.
  • The 5 topologies = "My Sister Bought Red Heels" → Mesh, Star, Bus, Ring, Hybrid.
  • Simplex-Half-Full = 1-way · 1-at-a-time · both-at-once.

§ 08 Critical Questions

Conceptual

  1. Define the Physical Layer in a single sentence. What is its data unit?
  2. List the four duties of the Physical Layer as specified by the slide.
  3. Differentiate between simplex, half-duplex, and full-duplex transmission modes with one real-world example each.
  4. Why is synchronization of bits a Physical Layer responsibility and not a Data Link Layer one?
  5. Explain why the Physical Layer is said to be "medium-agnostic in function but medium-specific in implementation."

Short-Answer

  1. Derive the number of physical links and per-node I/O ports for a mesh of 8 devices.
  2. State two advantages and two disadvantages of the bus topology.
  3. Name three standards organisations that publish Physical Layer standards.
  4. Define broadband as given in the slides.
  5. Compare circuit switching and packet switching in one row each of: path, bandwidth allocation, efficiency, example.

Diagram-Based

  1. Draw a mesh, star, bus, ring, and hybrid topology for 5 nodes. Label each.
  2. Draw the encapsulation stack showing exactly where the bit stream enters the physical medium. Label the PDU at each layer.
  3. Sketch packet-switched communication between host A and host B via three routers, showing that packets may take different paths.

Scenario-Based

  1. A company wires its new building using one long coaxial cable that taps every floor. Every floor goes offline when the basement cable is cut. Identify the topology, justify it with a slide-drawn disadvantage, and recommend an alternative.
  2. You are handed two devices: Device X regenerates an electrical signal but cannot read MAC addresses. Device Y reads MAC addresses and forwards frames. Classify each into its OSI layer and justify in one sentence.
  3. A remote office complains that large downloads arrive but take far longer than the sender thinks they should. Upstream speed tests show 20 Mbps down but only 1 Mbps up. Which Physical Layer property from the slides explains this asymmetry?
  4. Two routers are connected by an RS-232 console cable for out-of-band management. Identify the Physical Layer standard, the applicable transmission mode, and why this connection is not suitable for production data traffic.
